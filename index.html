02 / Understand the game

A bout, in plain English.

Two fencers face each other on a strip, also called a piste. The aim is to land valid touches while managing distance and defending yourself.

Explore competition basics ↗
01

Wait for the referee.

“En garde” means take your position. “Ready?” checks both fencers. “Fence!” starts the action; “Halt!” means stop immediately.

02

A light isn’t always a point.

Electronic equipment registers touches. Target area and weapon rules determine whether a point is awarded.

03

Learn priority with your coach.

In foil and sabre, right-of-way helps decide who scores when both hit. A correctly executed attack, parry, and response all matter.

04

The format sets the finish.

Common competition bouts go to 5 or 15 touches, with time limits. Beginner lessons may use shorter practice bouts.

03 / Build your foundation

Good fencing starts with your feet.

Start slowly and follow your coach’s demonstrations. Balance comes before speed.

↔

En garde

Stand side-on with your weapon-side foot forward, knees comfortably bent, and weight balanced.

→

Advance

Move your front foot forward, then follow with the back foot. Keep your stance steady.

←

Retreat

Move the back foot first, then the front foot. Stay balanced as you create distance.

⤴

Lunge

Extend your weapon arm and push from the back leg to reach forward. Learn the recovery with a coach.

What protective equipment is used?

A fencing mask, jacket, underarm protector (plastron), glove, fencing trousers, and long socks are standard protection. Chest protection requirements depend on the fencer and rules. Electric fencing also uses weapon-specific scoring gear and body cords. Ask your coach about the exact kit and standards for your weapon, age group, and event.

05 / Speak the language

Six words you’ll hear on day one.

Strip / piste

The long, narrow area where a bout takes place.

Touch

A hit registered or judged under the weapon’s rules.

Parry

A defensive blade action that blocks an attack.

Riposte

An offensive action after successfully parrying.

Distance

The space between you and your opponent.

Salute

A respectful greeting with the weapon before and after fencing.

A few first-time questions.

Do I need to be fit before starting?

You can build fitness as you learn. Tell the coach about your experience and any limitations so they can adapt the session.

Which weapon should I start with?

Start with the beginner program your club offers. If you have a choice, try a lesson in each and see which style you enjoy.

Does fencing hurt?

Touches can feel like a tap and may sometimes leave a bruise. Proper protection, controlled actions, and following your coach’s safety instructions are essential.

Am I too old to start?

Adults can begin fencing too. Ask nearby clubs about adult beginner groups and suitable session intensity.
